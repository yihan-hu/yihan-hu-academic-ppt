# Narrative Patterns

## Conference talk

Goal: one memorable scientific message with enough evidence to trust it.

Typical structure:
1. problem / gap;
2. research question;
3. minimal methods needed to trust the result;
4. 2-4 core results;
5. interpretation;
6. limitations;
7. take-home.

Use lower density and stronger takeaway titles. Move secondary methods to backup slides.

## Seminar / lab talk

Goal: show the reasoning process, not just the final paper.

Typical structure:
1. background;
2. prior evidence;
3. question or replication target;
4. data and methods;
5. result;
6. discrepancy / surprise;
7. possible explanations;
8. additional analyses;
9. current interpretation;
10. open questions.

Negative results are allowed and often central to the story.

## PhD defense

Goal: demonstrate a coherent research program and methodological command.

Recommended structure:
1. thesis-level background and conceptual model;
2. overview of Study I-IV or equivalent;
3. for each study: question -> data/methods -> core results -> conclusion;
4. cross-study synthesis;
5. strengths and limitations;
6. implications and future work;
7. final take-home.

Use repeated study navigation. Repeating layout patterns is desirable because the audience must track which study is active.

Do not overcompress methods or sensitivity analyses that may be discussed by the committee.

## Technical / methods talk

Goal: explain the estimand, model, assumptions, diagnostics, and interpretation.

Structure:
1. scientific problem;
2. estimand / target quantity;
3. data structure;
4. model architecture;
5. assumptions;
6. estimation procedure;
7. diagnostics / validation;
8. result;
9. sensitivity analysis;
10. limitations.

Dense specification slides are acceptable if visually structured.

## Scientific investigation

Use when the research story develops through contradiction or failed replication.

Pattern:

`Prior evidence -> Replication attempt -> Different result -> Conflict -> Candidate explanation -> New test -> Updated explanation`

Useful status labels:
- PUBLISHED;
- OUR DATA;
- DISCREPANCY;
- HYPOTHESIS;
- TEST;
- UPDATED VIEW.

This pattern should feel like reasoning, not marketing. Do not manufacture suspense by hiding known limitations.

## Result sequencing

For a complex figure, prefer:

`Full evidence -> Focus -> Interpretation`

For multiple studies, prefer:

`Question -> Design -> Result -> Meaning`

For a methods-heavy analysis, prefer:

`Why -> Model -> Diagnostics -> Result`

## Slide titles

Prefer declarative scientific titles when supported:
- "Hospital-treated infections were more frequent after diagnosis"
- "The association was concentrated in progressive disease"
- "NfL trajectories converged after treatment switching"

Use question titles when evidence is being introduced rather than concluded:
- "Does the association differ by treatment?"
- "Can cohort composition explain the discrepancy?"

Avoid generic titles such as "Results" when a more informative title fits.
